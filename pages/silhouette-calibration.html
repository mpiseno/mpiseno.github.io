Calibrating a wrist camera from pre-recorded monocular RGB

20 September 2026

Large datasets are messy

Most robot datasets include monocular RGB wrist videos. Some datasets, such as DROID, ship with camera extrinsics, which allow us to compute derived annotations such as 3D scene geometry or 2D masks. In Cloak for example, we used the wrist camera extrinsics to compute gripper masks for visual attention masking.

The problem is real datasets get messy at scale. Different rigs have camera mounts attached slightly differently, cameras accidentally flipped upside down, or use different gripper models (all of these problems are present in the DROID data). These all contribute to inaccurate camera extrinsics, even in a very standardized rig like DROID.

The standard fix is to recalibrate the cameras using hand-eye calibration. But this needs known point correspondences and access to the physical robot, which we don't have in a pre-recorded dataset. In Cloak, to calibrate wrist camera extrinsics in a pre-recorded monocular RGB video, we introduced a method we call Silhoutte Calibration; we render end-effector masks in sim and solve for the 6-dof camera extrinsics by regressing the mask on a pseudo-ground-truth target mask.

Silhoutte Calibration

We leverage the fact that the end-effector is roughly the same color and occupies approximately the same pixels in every wrist image. This allows us to compute a pseudo-ground-truth target mask composed of the "most constant" pixels across the trajectory.

For each episode with wrist frames \( I_t \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3} \) and joint positions \( q_t \), we want the pose of the camera in the end-effector frame, \( T_{ec} \in SE(3) \), which we parameterize as \( \theta \in \mathbb{R}^6 \) representing the 6-dof pose of the camera. We will solve the following optimization problem

\[ \begin{equation} \theta^* = \underset{\theta}{\arg\min} \; 1 - \text{IoU}\left( M(\theta, q_{t_0}), \; M_{gt} \right) \label{eq:opt} \end{equation} \]
where \( M(\theta, q_{t_0}) \) is the end-effector mask rendered as a function of the robot configuration and camera pose and \( M_{gt} \) is the pseudo-ground-truth mask.

We construct the pseudo-ground-truth mask in the following way. We take frames where the end-effector is in a known pose (e.g. gripper open), coverting the frames to grayscale, and cropping them to a region where the gripper is known to be (e.g. the bottom half of the image). Call these frames \( I_t \) with pixel values \( I_{t, p} \) indexed by \( p \). In DROID the grippers are all the black Robotiq-2f85 gripper, so we want to find the most consistently dark pixels. To do this we compute the median intensity across time for each pixel and then another spatial median. Similarly, to find the most constant pixels in the episode (regardless of color) we compute the median of the temporal standard deviaton.

\[ \begin{gather} s_{int} = \underset{p}{\text{median}} \left( \underset{t}{\text{median}} \; I_{t, p} \right) \\ s_{std} = \underset{p}{\text{median}} \left( \underset{t}{\text{std}} \; I_{t, p} \right) \end{gather} \]
then the pseudo-ground-truth mask is computed as the intersection of intensity and standard deviation masks.
\[ \begin{gather} M_{int} = \mathbb{1}[I_0 < s_{int}] \\ M_{std} = \mathbb{1}[I_0 < s_{std}] \\ M_{gt} = M_{int} \cap M_{std} \label{eq:mgt} \end{gather} \]

The pseudo-ground-truth is often noisy, and not suitable for direct use in Cloak, but is a sufficient target for optimizing \( \theta \). We solve Equation \( \ref{eq:opt} \) with Nelder-Mead initialized at the median DROID camera extrinsic vector. Figure shows qualitative results of Silhoutte Calibration on several episodes.

3. DROID results

DROID ships its own wrist extrinsics for each episode, obtained from hand-eye calibration. We compare those against ours on a fixed random subset of 200 episodes that spans the labs.

In the video, the green outline stays on the fingers through the whole motion, including the grasp, even though it was fit on a single frame with the gripper open. The cyan outline, rendered from DROID's pose, is visibly off.

For these three episodes the two poses differ by 25 to 37 mm and 2 to 3.5°. These are the largest differences among the grasping episodes in the subset, not typical ones.

DROID's poses scatter across episodes (a standard deviation of 3.7 / 3.7 / 7.6 mm) while ours cluster (1.6 / 1.1 / 1.0 mm). The rigs are nominally identical, so a tight cluster is what we should expect. However, the tightness shows consistency, not accuracy. Every fit starts from the same pose, and as discussed below, the starting pose influences where the fit ends up.

4. Limitations and release

The main limitation is that a silhouette does not pin down all 6 parameters. If we perturb the starting pose by about 10 mm and 5°, the fit does not return: it ends up a median of 7 mm and 3.5° away at an IoU cost of only 0.01. The ambiguity is two dimensional. A vertical shift of the camera trades against pitch, and a sideways shift trades against yaw. At 320×180 the difference in parallax is smaller than the noise in the target, and adding frames with the gripper closed does not help. So initialization matters, which is why we start from DROID's median pose. Other limitations: we use a single shared focal length, the episode needs frames with the gripper open, and the gripper model has to match the real gripper. In our case the Robotiq coupling was misplaced by 4 mm in the XML, and every rendered silhouette was off by that much until we fixed it.
